RUINS · RUNES · SALVAGE

Briefing

The Dirt Diggers

Scrappers live where other people see only rubble.

Collapsed cities, flooded basements, buried factories, forgotten tunnels, wrecked vehicles, and mountains of corroding machinery are their hunting grounds. Beneath centuries of dust lie the remnants of the Bygones: pumps, engines, weapons, tools, electronics, metals, and artifacts whose original purposes may have been forgotten but whose value remains enormous.

A Scrapper does not need to understand everything he finds.

He needs to recognize what might be valuable, survive long enough to recover it, and know who will pay.

Born in the Ruins

The first Scrappers appeared immediately after the Eshaton.

Civilization collapsed, but its machinery remained scattered through the ruins. Settlements needed generators, pumps, tools, spare parts, and anything else that might keep a fragment of the old world functioning.

Someone had to go out and find them.

Those early scavengers entered structures weakened by impact, crossed plains buried beneath ash, descended into basements filled with gas, and handled machines that could still kill centuries before anyone called them artifacts.

Many died.

Those who survived learned how ruins behaved. They learned where floors collapsed, where water gathered, how old buildings had been organized, and where desperate Bygones might have hidden valuables before the end.

Eventually, the ruins stopped being hostile territory.

They became home.

Life in the Wasteland

European Scrappers are famous for their independence.

A Scrapper may spend weeks alone among ruined buildings with little more than a weapon, tools, water, and whatever food can be carried. Settlements offer warmth and trade, but they also contain rules, arguments, taxes, obligations, and people asking questions.

The ruins are simpler.

A wall either holds or it does not. A tunnel is safe or it is not. Something buried beneath the floor is valuable or useless. Hunger and thirst are honest problems.

Years of living this way create people who are often quiet, suspicious, territorial, and extraordinarily capable of surviving where ordinary travelers would die.

Many eventually prefer ruins to civilization.

Reading the Dead World

Successful Scrappers do more than dig.

A ruined building tells a story. Its entrances reveal how people once moved through it. Broken walls indicate where upper floors collapsed. Pipes suggest water systems. Heavy cables may lead toward generators, workshops, or machinery.

A skilled Scrapper learns to reconstruct the Bygone world from fragments.

Who lived here? What was this building used for? Where would equipment have been stored? Did its occupants abandon it before the Eshaton, flee afterward, or die inside?

Such instincts separate someone who collects rusty metal from someone who finds artifacts.

Experience is often worth more than any map.

Runes

European Scrappers may travel alone, but they still communicate.

Across the ruins they scratch simple runes into walls, doors, stones, and wreckage. Personal symbols identify who has passed through. Arrows mark routes. Jagged signs warn of danger. Other marks indicate water, shelter, traps, or places already searched.

A rune can save someone's life.

It can also announce ownership.

Experienced Scrappers spread their marks over enormous territories, and famous signs become recognized far beyond their creator's home. Some even tattoo their personal rune onto their hand or forehead.

There is no universal Scrapper alphabet. Meanings shift between regions, and a visitor who assumes too much may discover that a familiar-looking symbol means something very different fifty kilometers away.

African Scrappers generally do not share this tradition. Their expeditions remain far more communal, and spoken communication replaces the lonely messages scratched by Europeans into concrete.

Mouse, Badger, and Fox

Many Scrapper careers begin unpleasantly.

Mice are often children working beneath older Scrappers. Their small bodies make them useful for squeezing through collapsed passages, narrow shafts, and openings adults cannot enter. Some are orphans; others have simply fallen into the hands of someone who considers a child another piece of equipment.

Survivors become Badgers and begin working alone.

Experience transforms some into Foxes: skilled ruin hunters who know their territory intimately, maintain hidden stores, and understand where worthwhile salvage is likely to remain.

Beyond them are Lone Wolves, seasoned artifact hunters whose knowledge, weapons, and instincts make them dangerous competitors.

At the far end stands the Cave Bear, a veteran so adapted to the wasteland that his territory may be filled with hidden passages, traps, caches, and defenses. When a Cave Bear marks a place as forbidden, sensible people go somewhere else.

Mechanists and Manufacturers

Some Scrappers stop treating artifacts merely as goods.

Mechanists take them apart.

Gears, springs, coils, housings, plates, wiring, lenses, and engines become raw material for new machines. A Mechanist may not understand every principle the Bygones mastered, but practical experimentation can still produce firearms, traps, locks, generators, pumps, vehicles, and devices no village craftsman could duplicate.

Manufacturers take this further. They maintain workshops filled with tools, accumulated knowledge, and specialized machinery. Other Manufacturers may share access to their workshops, exchanging techniques in a loose network of craftsmen.

Settlements value them enormously.

Chroniclers are less comfortable. Every artifact rebuilt by a Scrapper is one that was not immediately surrendered to an Alcove.

Scavengers

Individualism has limits.

Some artifacts are simply too large for one person to recover.

Scavengers therefore work in groups, pooling labor and heavy equipment. Tractor rigs, winches, pneumatic cutters, cranes, and improvised machinery allow them to retrieve objects that a lone Scrapper could only stare at.

Their leaders are known as Alpha Wolves.

An Alpha Wolf earns loyalty through work rather than ceremony. A good one knows how to keep a crew alive, arrange transport, protect the haul, and find buyers without surrendering half the profit to someone who never entered the ruins.

For Scrappers accustomed to trusting no one, that sort of cooperation is remarkable.

Chroniclers and Drafts

Most European Scrappers eventually return to the Chroniclers.

Exchange Alcoves purchase artifacts with Drafts or information about other potential finds. Chroniclers inspect whatever is placed before them, determine its value according to standards they rarely explain, and make an offer.

Haggling is not encouraged.

Scrappers commonly suspect the Chroniclers know far more about an artifact's real importance than they admit. Unfortunately, the Chroniclers also provide one of the largest reliable markets for technological salvage.

The relationship is therefore mutually dependent and deeply unequal.

Scrappers bring fragments of the old world out of places Chroniclers would rather not personally enter.

Chroniclers decide what those fragments are worth.

The African Scrappers

African Scrappers developed differently.

After the Eshaton, much of the technology of Africa's great coastal cities vanished beneath the Mediterranean. Salvagers eventually crossed the sea and discovered that shattered Europe contained industrial wealth on an extraordinary scale.

Factories were dismantled. Generators, tools, steel, and machinery were shipped south. African reconstruction accelerated while European ruins were stripped piece by piece.

African Scrappers still travel north in search of technology, but they often do so as members of expeditions rather than solitary wanderers. Their comrades become surrogate family. Success enriches their home communities, and evenings in camp are filled with food, stories, arguments, laughter, and homesickness rather than the isolation preferred by many Europeans.

Neolibyans fill the commercial role that Chroniclers do in Borca.

African Scrappers prefer Dinars.

Bedain and Tech-Central

Two places exert enormous influence over Scrapper life.

Bedain is the world's greatest wrecking port. Mountains of ruined ships and scrap surround workshops capable of constructing everything from rifles and gearboxes to enormous machinery. African master craftsmen take apprentices, Surge Tanks are built and maintained, and Scrappers from distant lands come simply to say they have walked its alleys.

Leaving one's rune upon the wall at Syracuse is a mark of prestige recognized far beyond the island.

Tech-Central in Justitian represents another kind of Scrapper world. Workshops, exchange Alcoves, merchants, mechanics, scavengers, and the endless flow of artifacts make it one of Europe's most important centers for salvage.

Unfortunately, someone else has noticed how much wealth passes through it.

The Cartel

Bosch's Scrapper Cartel has become a growing power in West Borca.

Its people monitor ruin fields, demand payment for access, pressure independent Scrappers, and place Appraisers outside Chronicler Alcoves. An Appraiser offers to negotiate a better price for someone's artifacts—and takes a percentage for the service.

Refusing can be unhealthy.

Cartel Thugs surrender part of their income in return for protection and access to controlled ruins. Appraisers collect percentages and negotiate with Chroniclers. Officers stand above them and answer ultimately to Bosch.

For some Scrappers, the Cartel provides organization, security, and bargaining power against the Chroniclers.

For others, it is merely another parasite demanding a share of something it did not dig out of the earth.

Legends

Scrappers respect accomplishment more than titles.

A person's rune can become famous long before their name does. Stories spread around campfires of impossible expeditions, hidden vaults, surviving traps, lost caches, and artifacts carried home from places everyone else considered death sentences.

Those whose exploits become known across the wasteland may become Legends.

A Legend requires no office. Other Scrappers recognize the rune, repeat the stories, and may follow simply because the person has proved capable of finding treasure and returning alive.

Among people who distrust authority, reputation is the closest thing to command.

The Scrapper Conflict

Scrappers keep civilization alive by tearing apart the civilization that came before it.

They recover pumps for settlements, weapons for armies, machines for merchants, and artifacts for Chroniclers. Mechanists transform dead technology into something useful again. Yet every ruin stripped clean is also a piece of history removed forever.

Independence creates another contradiction.

European Scrappers pride themselves on needing no one, yet most eventually depend upon Chroniclers to buy their discoveries. Scavengers discover that cooperation can accomplish what solitude cannot. Cartel members surrender freedom in exchange for protection and bargaining power.

Even the search itself becomes a trap.

There is always another buried chamber, another promising ruin, another hidden cache. Many Scrappers continue long after their bodies tell them to stop, convinced that the next excavation will contain the find that changes everything.

Often, no one knows when they finally die.

One day their rune simply stops appearing on the walls.
