TRADITION · PROSPERITY · SURVIVAL

Culture & Society

The Lion Rampant

Africa presents a striking contrast to the fractured societies across the Mediterranean. Its coastal cities celebrate wealth, hospitality, craftsmanship, and confidence. Markets overflow with spices, fine fabrics, jewelry, weapons, medicines, and machinery recovered from Europe. Ships carry merchants and expedition crews between bustling ports, while enormous Surge Tanks advertise the power of their owners. Public gardens, fountains, promenades, and workshops turn commercial success into something everyone can see.

Yet this prosperity tells only part of the story. Beyond the cities lie indebted villages, plantations worked by slaves, independent communities resisting the merchant Cult, and jungles that conceal ancient machines. Across the interior, the Psychovores consume land and alter the people living near them. Africa's strength is real, but so are the pressures threatening to undo it.

The Time of the Crow

Before the Eshaton, exploitation and warfare scarred the continent. Foreign corporations exchanged weapons for access to resources, local warlords fought over the profits, and heavily fortified states guarded their borders. The arrival of HIVE transformed existing misery into catastrophe. Victims developed necrotic lesions and died as the disease spread through communities with no effective treatment.

Rumors of a European cure drove desperate populations north. Refugees crossed deserts and overwhelmed coastal defenses, then attempted the Mediterranean passage in overloaded boats and improvised rafts. European warships blocked their escape. Whether Europeans had caused the disease mattered less to those dying than the belief that salvation existed across the water and was being withheld. Memories of abandonment became part of the continent's understanding of Europe: the Crow had prospered while Africa suffered.

Dhoruba and Rebirth

On March 13, 2073, the Eshaton ended that old order. A celestial fragment passing through the atmosphere drove a devastating blast across Central Africa, leaving the immense scar known as the Dhoruba. Surviving communities emerged into a continent emptied by disease and destruction. Mountain herders, oasis dwellers, and peoples with immunity to HIVE preserved fragments of life through shared labor, faith, and mutual dependence.

Over the following centuries, climate change transformed the landscape. Cooler temperatures and Atlantic moisture brought rain to regions once dominated by desert. Riverbeds filled, savannas spread, and jungles expanded. Africa became fertile in ways its ancestors could scarcely have imagined. Surviving oil reserves supplied another foundation for recovery, allowing transport, industry, and trade to flourish alongside agriculture.

The Heart, Claws, and Soul

African identity draws together numerous peoples and traditions beneath the symbol of the Lion. This unity does not mean that every village shares the same customs. Family histories, tribal practices, local loyalties, and ancestral obligations remain important. Nevertheless, common principles increasingly connect communities that once considered themselves separate.

Three Cults embody the Lion's complementary strengths. Neolibyans are its heart, sustaining prosperity through exploration, diplomacy, and commerce. Scourgers are its claws, defending communities, avenging injuries, and enforcing African power abroad. Anubians are its soul, interpreting spiritual obligations and guiding humanity's relationship with life, death, and destiny. Together, these roles provide an influential model for African society, although independent Clans and other Cults do not always accept the authority exercised in the Lion's name.

Ancestors and the Living World

For many Africans, ancestors remain present in daily life. Trees, stones, pools, and unusual formations may house spirits whose goodwill must be maintained. Small figurines, woven ribbons, offerings of milk, and other acts of respect acknowledge the relationship between people and the powers surrounding them. Hunting an animal or cutting down a tree can require justification and ritual observance.

Anubians give this spiritual world structure, but Scourgers also preserve traditions and offer guidance when no Anubian is present. Such practices belong within ordinary community life rather than a separate sphere of worship. Markets, family celebrations, commercial agreements, and journeys can all carry spiritual significance. Even wealthy merchants seeking long-term contracts may ask Anubians to examine the Threads of Life of those involved before committing themselves.

Trade and the Bank of Commerce

Tripol stands at the center of Africa's commercial world. Built upon the remains of Tripoli, it attracts merchants, travelers, artisans, and tribes eager to participate in its prosperity. Its streets combine ancestral statues and cultural treasures with cables, engines, towers, bazaars, and the machinery of an expanding economy. Neolibyan expeditions leave its ports to explore distant coasts and bring back goods, artifacts, technology, and opportunities.

Above this activity stands the Bank of Commerce. Its concessions determine who may exploit particular routes, plantations, oil fields, and commercial sectors. Its archives, maps, wealth, and lending power make it Africa's most powerful institution. Credit can transform a village, but compound interest can also bind entire communities to obligations they cannot escape. Prosperity spreads through family networks; dependency spreads through contracts. Even successful Neolibyans operate within a system capable of financing their ambitions or ending them.

The Lion and the Crow

Hostility toward Europeans hardened after the Hybrispanian Conquistadores invaded Africa and devastated recovering cities. African victory did not end at the coastline. Scourgers pursued the defeated invaders across Gibraltar, and captives were brought back to rebuild what had been destroyed. Over time, vengeance became an enduring justification for conquest, forced labor, and European enslavement.

Relations across the Mediterranean consequently mix commerce with fear, resentment, and domination. Europeans may be customers, trading partners, captives, enemies, or rare accepted guests. Individual Africans do not all share the same attitudes, but the remembered crimes of the Crow give those relationships a historical weight that ordinary negotiation cannot easily erase.

Slavery and Resistance

Slave labor supports ports, plantations, workshops, and the construction of great machines. Captives from Hybrispania and the Balkhan unload ships, cultivate fields, repair vehicles, and perform exhausting work beneath the tropical sun. Meanwhile, African communities burdened by debt may lose meaningful independence without ever being captured in war. Some send their children on salvage expeditions, hoping a valuable discovery will buy the village's freedom.

Resistance takes several forms. In Tunis, enslaved people seized a Bygone arsenal during a pirate diversion and established an armed enclave that Scourgers have struggled to reconquer. Elsewhere, Madame Dayo and the secret network known as the Road help European slaves escape toward home. These movements expose disagreement within Africa itself: shared identity does not guarantee shared approval of the institutions sustaining its wealth.

The Psychovores

The Dhoruba breeds an alien vegetation known as the Psychovores, or Spirit Eaters. Their forms are unnervingly geometric, their fruits poisonous, and their thorns capable of causing devastating tissue decay. They displace ordinary plants, engulf settlements, obstruct roads, and extend into waterways and the sea. Burning or poisoning them offers no dependable solution, because attacks provoke further adaptation.

Their influence also reaches into language and identity. Communities near the plants find old linguistic boundaries dissolving into an intuitive, emotionally charged form of communication. Whispers are interpreted as ancestral voices, while some people abandon their villages and disappear into the vegetation. Apparent unity therefore comes at a disturbing price: distinct traditions may be fading under an influence no one fully understands. Anubians can enter places that kill others and return carrying seeds for extraordinary preparations, but their relationship with the plants remains mysterious.

Qabis and Anubia

The growing threat has forced the Bank of Commerce to seek Spitalian assistance. In Qabis, a group of doctors has been accepted through a Scourger-led ritual that gives them the standing of honorary Africans. Their presence challenges familiar assumptions: Europeans can walk freely, practice medicine, take in foundlings, and participate in local life while researching the enemy advancing through the jungle.

Their base occupies the Surge Tank Aesculapius, while another vessel, Ndulu, has carried an expedition into the Psychovores. Its reported movement toward Anubia raises suspicion despite the alliance. Around Cairo, the vegetation has already conquered the surrounding country. The Forbidden City is said to conceal the Anubians' deepest knowledge within its pyramids, but outsiders cannot readily separate reliable information from legend or deliberate secrecy.

Beyond the Merchant Cities

Africa's hinterland contains thriving estates, exploited plantations, isolated villages, and communities that prefer their own customs to metropolitan grandeur. Rivers fed by the Ahaggar, Air, and Tibesti highlands sustain enormous expanses of fertile country. Much of this interior remains poorly understood, and the jungle can be as formidable a barrier as any armed patrol.

Not everyone accepts Neolibyan commercial supremacy. The Leopards form a loose alliance of independent Clans that trade outside the concession system, use secret inland routes, and sail where larger merchants hesitate to venture. Their success demonstrates that African prosperity need not pass through the Bank. Attempts to suppress them trouble even Scourgers, whose duty to protect African tradition conflicts with demands to attack fellow Africans.

Old Machines and New Horrors

The jungle also conceals concrete fortresses, flooded facilities, and AMSUMOs: Bygone combat machines known as Machine Men. Some remain motionless for extraordinary periods before suddenly returning to violent activity. Others imitate creatures or humans they encounter, making their behavior difficult to predict. Their durable components attract hunters despite the terrible cost of recovering them.

Where Psychovores meet the influence of Earth Chakras, Discordance produces stranger dangers. Cocoons form within the vegetation and release bizarre organisms, many of which quickly die in the surrounding environment. Some survive long enough to be captured and exhibited by hunters such as the Shabath Clan. These encounters reveal an Africa still being transformed by forces that wealth, tradition, and military strength cannot fully control.

The African Conflict

Africa's defining struggle is the tension between confidence and vulnerability. Its people have survived catastrophe, rebuilt cities, renewed old traditions, and achieved a unity much of Europe lacks. Yet the Bank can turn development into dependency, ancestral vengeance can justify exploitation, and the Psychovores can make cultural togetherness inseparable from the loss of cultural distinction.

An African character may therefore be proud of the Lion while questioning what is done in its name. Loyalty to family can conflict with obligations to creditors; respect for ancestors can collide with evidence of alien influence; distrust of Europeans can obstruct an alliance necessary for survival. Across cities and villages alike, the question is how to preserve the continent's achievements without surrendering the people, freedoms, and traditions that gave those achievements meaning.
