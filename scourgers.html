Briefing

Avengers of the Dark Continent

If Africa is the Lion, the Scourgers are its claws.

They are the continent's warrior Cult: fighters, guardians of tradition, raiders, judges, and living symbols of an Africa determined never to be conquered again. Where Neolibyans negotiate and Anubians interpret the unseen, Scourgers answer danger through discipline and violence.

Training never ends. Scourgers run, climb, spar to first blood, and practice relentlessly with spear, rifle, shield, and Scourge. Physical excellence honors the ancestors and proves the warrior is ready when Africa calls.

The Lion and the Crow

Scourger identity is inseparable from Africa's memory of conquest.

Their traditions preserve centuries of slavery, colonial rule, exploitation, and violence by Europeans. After the Eshaton, Africans rebuilt while shattered Europe seemed too weak to threaten them. That certainty ended when Hybrispanian Conquistadores crossed into Africa, destroyed settlements, and drove toward Tripol.

The Africans eventually drove them back across Gibraltar. Scourgers followed, carried the war into Hybrispania, and brought captives back to rebuild what had been destroyed.

From that history grew the Crow: the white conqueror, predatory and dangerous if allowed to regain strength. Many Scourgers therefore regard Europeans not merely as former enemies but as a recurring threat that must remain restrained.

That belief creates the Cult's central contradiction. A people formed by resistance to oppression now enslave others in the name of preventing oppression from returning.

Scourgers call it balance.

The Eight Ancestors

Scourgers believe spirits exist beside the living world. Stones, animals, plants, places, and the dead all possess forces deserving respect, but spirits normally do not interfere directly in human affairs.

The Eight Ancestors are the great exception.

When Africa stood near ruin, tradition says the Eight reached across the boundary between worlds and strengthened the Lion. Their power flowed through warriors across the continent, uniting peoples who shared neither tribe nor language. Those chosen warriors became the Scourgers.

Eight ancestral masks recur throughout African tradition. Their differing natures may guide, inspire, challenge, or corrupt, but worth must always be demonstrated through action.

Guardians of Tradition

Scourgers protect more than borders. African communities call upon them to settle disputes, punish wrongdoing, and preserve custom.

A liar may be forbidden to speak until the village spirits send a sign, while someone who causes damage may be required to repair it personally. Their judgments arise from tradition, local custom, and ancestral authority rather than a written code.

Anubians command particular respect in these matters. Scourgers commonly defer to their deeper understanding of ancestors and the spiritual order; without one, a Chaga may preserve the rites himself.

Warui

Africa contains countless languages, yet Scourgers from distant regions can fight together with remarkable unity. They attribute this to Warui, "from the river."

Warui is a primal form of communication built from simple words, sounds, clicks, rhythm, and emotional intensity. Scourgers understand it as a current flowing from the Eight Ancestors, carrying images and feelings more directly than ordinary speech.

Within Africa it binds warriors who share no native tongue. Farther north, the connection weakens, and some chew Psychovore seeds to strengthen it.

Becoming a Scourger

Joining the Cult is not simply a matter of ambition. Tradition gives the mother decisive authority over whether her child is offered to the Scourgers.

Around the age of twelve comes the true test. Initiation differs by region: one candidate may be abandoned in dangerous wilderness with only a spear; another may be required to hunt a released captive. Whatever the form, the trial is deliberately life-threatening, and other Scourgers do not intervene merely because the candidate is losing.

Survival means the ancestors have accepted the initiate.

Once recognized, the young Scourger is supported by the home community and may draw upon Neolibyan resources. Until roughly twenty-eight, warriors are discouraged from marriage; survivors who become elders increasingly shift toward judgment and planning.

Pack and Rank

A Scourger belongs to the pack before himself.

New warriors begin as Dufus, mocked and challenged until they learn that surviving initiation did not make them great. They scout dangerous ground, act as bait, fight in the first line, and master spear and shield.

Proven fighters may become Hondos, ancestral warriors permitted to carry firearms. Those whose strength lies in planning become Damus, the pack's scouts, drivers, and tacticians.

Above them stands the Chaga. He commands in battle, embodies ancestral authority, and carries responsibility for warriors lost under his leadership.

Every pack may also have a Simba, its Lion, who claims the strongest opponent and must face that enemy without assistance.

Standing apart from normal command is the Dumisai, the pack's living conscience. Only the Dumisai may openly challenge the Chaga's judgment, even through ritual combat.

Moyo and Kifo

A Dumisai's honor must eventually be tested.

He may select a Hondo or Damu as his Moyo, a ritual soul mate. The Moyo surrenders his mask and leaves the pack to complete eight heroic tasks alone within two moons. No Scourger may help.

Success confirms the Dumisai's honor. Failure makes both warriors Kifos.

Their masks are broken and the pack casts them out. Some Kifos become mercenaries or assassins; others join Hybrispanians or Voivodes and fight former comrades. Exile severs warrior, pack, and ancestors at once.

Neolibyans and Anubians

Africa's three great Cults describe themselves through the Lion: Neolibyans are its heart, Scourgers its claws, and Anubians its soul.

At home, even low-ranking Scourgers may enjoy more admiration than wealthy Neolibyans. Merchants provide weapons, ammunition, vehicles, Petro, food, and lodging, while Scourgers mock them as soft people who profit while others bleed. Outside Africa, Neolibyans control the expeditions, concessions, ports, and supply networks sustaining many Scourger campaigns.

Anubians occupy a different place. Scourgers commonly regard them as spiritual kin and accept their guidance.

The Lion needs all three, even when its heart, claws, and soul disagree.

Weapons and War

The Scourge itself is a symbol: a shock whip whose imagery reverses the weapon once used against Africans and places it in African hands.

Scourgers also use spears, shields, machetes, assault rifles, grenades, nets, and bolas. Neolibyan arsenals provide much of this equipment. Koms give packs mobility across rough terrain, while trained hyenas track enemies where vehicles cannot go.

Their combat culture prizes speed, nerve, aggression, and personal initiative within the discipline of the pack.

Hybrispania

Nowhere is Scourger ideology tested more brutally than Al-Andalus.

Generations of Scourgers have fought to hold African territory in southern Hybrispania and push Guerreros deeper into the Warpage. Ambush answers reprisal, settlements burn, and each death becomes justification for another.

Scourgers see the war through ancestral memory: descendants of invaders are being prevented from threatening Africa again. Hybrispanians see foreign soldiers occupying their homeland.

Neolibyans increasingly recognize that endless war damages trade, while many Scourgers regard retreat as betrayal of everyone who died bringing the Lion this far.

The victory over conquest has become a war over whether conquest can be justified by what came before it.

The Scourger Conflict

Scourgers embody qualities Africa genuinely admires: courage, endurance, loyalty, discipline, reverence for ancestors, and willingness to place themselves between their people and danger.

They also inherit a worldview built from historical trauma and maintained through violence.

A warrior may sincerely believe slavery restores balance. Another may begin to recognize the same cruelty in his own side that ancestral stories condemned in the Crow. A Chaga may be asked to defend a Neolibyan venture he considers dishonorable. A Simba may meet a worthy European opponent and discover respect where hatred was expected.

Every Scourger is taught to ask whether an action honors the ancestors.

The harder question is whether the ancestors would recognize what their descendants have become.
